Stability

Stability

One-sentence definition

Stability describes whether specified small perturbations of a dynamical object produce evolutions that remain close under a declared time direction and notion of closeness.

Why this concept matters

“Stable” is incomplete unless the object, perturbation, time horizon, and comparison criterion are stated. At this stage LDG focuses on forward Lyapunov stability of a fixed point and distinguishes it from asymptotic return.

Intuition

Balance a ball in a bowl and nudge it slightly. If it stays near the bottom, the equilibrium is stable. If it also returns toward the bottom, it is asymptotically stable. A ball balanced on a hilltop is a fixed point but not stable.

Formal definition

Let \(x^\ast\) be a fixed point of a forward evolution on a state space \(\mathcal X\). In neighborhood form, \(x^\ast\) is Lyapunov stable when, for every neighborhood \(U\) of \(x^\ast\), there exists a neighborhood \(V\subseteq U\) such that every trajectory starting in \(V\) is supported for all \(t\ge0\) and remains in \(U\) for all \(t\ge0\).

On a metric state space \((\mathcal X,d)\) this becomes

\[ \forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists\delta>0\;\forall x_0\in\mathcal X: \quad d(x_0,x^\ast)<\delta \Longrightarrow \left( [0,\infty)\subseteq I_{x_0} \;\text{ and }\; d(\phi_t(x_0),x^\ast)<\varepsilon\ \forall t\ge0 \right), \]

where \(I_{x_0}\) is the supported elapsed-time interval through \(x_0\).

The fixed point is asymptotically stable when it is Lyapunov stable and there is a neighborhood \(A\) of \(x^\ast\) such that every trajectory from \(A\) is supported for all forward time and

\[ \lim_{t\to\infty}d(\phi_t(x_0),x^\ast)=0. \]

Convergence without the Lyapunov condition is attraction but is not automatically asymptotic stability. Teschl explicitly notes that the convergence condition alone need not imply stability (Teschl 2012, sec. 6.5).

The fixed point is locally exponentially stable when there exist constants \(C\ge1\), \(\alpha>0\), and \(\delta>0\) such that, for all \(x_0\) with \(d(x_0,x^\ast)<\delta\), the forward trajectory is supported and

\[ d(\phi_t(x_0),x^\ast) \le C e^{-\alpha t}d(x_0,x^\ast), \qquad t\ge0. \]

This quantitative bound implies Lyapunov stability and local attraction under the stated metric and support assumptions (Teschl 2012, sec. 6.5).

Local and global scope

The definitions above are local because they constrain only sufficiently nearby initial states. Global asymptotic stability additionally requires the declared forward domain to include every admissible initial state and requires all of those trajectories to converge to \(x^\ast\), while retaining Lyapunov stability. “Global” is therefore relative to the stated state space and forward domain, not necessarily to all of \(\mathbb R^n\).

Which perturbation is being tested?

The bounded definitions perturb the initial state while holding the evolution rule, parameters, inputs, state description, and metric fixed. Parameter robustness, input-to-state stability, stochastic stability, structural stability, observation robustness, and numerical stability are different questions with different perturbation objects and criteria.

Coordinate and metric dependence

TipLDG Synthesis

The neighborhood definition of Lyapunov and asymptotic stability is preserved under a verified topological conjugacy that transports the evolution and neighborhoods. Exponential stability additionally contains a numerical rate and distance bound, so it is not invariant under an arbitrary homeomorphism or arbitrary rescaling without further regularity and metric-equivalence conditions. LDG therefore records the metric and coordinate convention for quantitative rate claims rather than treating \(C\) or \(\alpha\) as representation-free.

Core notation

  • \(x^\ast\): the fixed point whose stability is tested.
  • \(x_0\): a perturbed initial state.
  • \(d\): the declared notion of distance or closeness.
  • \(\varepsilon\): tolerated departure from the reference.
  • \(\delta\): allowed initial perturbation.
  • \(t\ge0\): the forward-time direction in scope.
  • \(A\): local basin neighborhood used in the asymptotic-stability definition.
  • \(C,\alpha\): multiplicative constant and exponential decay rate.
  • forward support/completeness: requirement that the quantified trajectories exist for all \(t\ge0\).

Prerequisites

Fixed Point identifies the invariant reference state. Flow supplies its evolution and the nearby trajectories being compared. Metric makes the quantitative notion of closeness explicit.

Assumed background: neighborhoods, limits, and elementary metric distance.

Relationship to adjacent concepts

Concept Distinction
Fixed point Fixedness concerns the unperturbed state; stability concerns nearby initial states.
Attraction Requires convergence toward a set. It need not imply Lyapunov stability; asymptotic stability requires both attraction and stability.
Global asymptotic stability Extends attraction to the declared full forward domain while retaining Lyapunov stability. It is stronger than a local statement.
Exponential stability Adds a metric-dependent quantitative decay bound; it is stronger than asymptotic stability in the stated setting.
Robustness Robustness may concern parameter, model, input, or observation perturbations rather than initial-state perturbations.
Numerical stability A property of an algorithm or discretization, not automatically of the underlying dynamical system.
Statistical stationarity A distributional time-invariance property, not Lyapunov stability.

What this does NOT imply

Stability does not imply:

  • asymptotic convergence unless that stronger condition is shown;
  • resilience to parameter changes, inputs, noise, or model misspecification;
  • global behavior from all initial conditions unless global scope and forward support are proved;
  • a unique equilibrium or basin;
  • that a finite observed trajectory will visibly return; or
  • that low variance in data establishes dynamical stability;
  • that successful decoding, low reconstruction error, or a smooth posterior mean verifies all nearby initial states and all future times; or
  • that stability transfers through a non-injective observation or projection.

Minimal example

Three scalar systems separate the principal cases:

  1. For \(\dot x=0\), every point is Lyapunov stable because nearby states remain where they started, but no point attracts a distinct neighboring state.
  2. For \(\dot x=-x\), \(x^\ast=0\) is exponentially stable because \(x(t)=x_0e^{-t}\) satisfies the decay bound with \(C=1\) and \(\alpha=1\).
  3. For \(\dot x=x\), \(x^\ast=0\) is unstable because arbitrarily small nonzero initial states grow in forward time.

These examples classify the stated systems directly. They do not authorize a general Jacobian or linearization criterion for nonlinear models; those theorem-level results remain outside this bounded Concept.

Neuroscience / behavioral relevance

Stability can formalize hypotheses about persistence or recovery of neural and behavioral states after perturbation. Evidence requires specified perturbations and state variables; repeatable occupancy near a region or low trial variance is not enough by itself.

Relevance to LDG

At L2, Stability supplies explicit quantifiers, time-support assumptions, local/global scope, and the hierarchy among Lyapunov, asymptotic, and exponential stability. It also forces analyses to state which perturbation, state space, metric, and coordinate convention define “nearby” and “rate.”

Open questions

  • Which perturbations are experimentally realizable and scientifically relevant?
  • How should stability be assessed when only partial noisy observations are available?
  • When is a local stability statement adequate for a behavioral claim?

References

  • Lyapunov-function, linearization, and eigenvalue criteria in later parts of Teschl are intentionally deferred from this Concept.

# Stability

## One-sentence definition

## Formal definition

Let $x^\ast$ be a fixed point of a forward evolution on a state space $\mathcal X$. In neighborhood form, $x^\ast$ is **Lyapunov stable** when, for every neighborhood $U$ of $x^\ast$, there exists a neighborhood $V\subseteq U$ such that every trajectory starting in $V$ is supported for all $t\ge0$ and remains in $U$ for all $t\ge0$.

On a metric state space $(\mathcal X,d)$ this becomes

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists\delta>0\;\forall x_0\in\mathcal X:
\quad
d(x_0,x^\ast)<\delta
\Longrightarrow
\left(
[0,\infty)\subseteq I_{x_0}
\;\text{ and }\;
d(\phi_t(x_0),x^\ast)<\varepsilon\ \forall t\ge0
\right),
$$

where $I_{x_0}$ is the supported elapsed-time interval through $x_0$.

The fixed point is **asymptotically stable** when it is Lyapunov stable and there is a neighborhood $A$ of $x^\ast$ such that every trajectory from $A$ is supported for all forward time and

$$
\lim_{t\to\infty}d(\phi_t(x_0),x^\ast)=0.
$$

Convergence without the Lyapunov condition is attraction but is not automatically asymptotic stability. Teschl explicitly notes that the convergence condition alone need not imply stability [@Teschl2012, sec. 6.5].

The fixed point is **locally exponentially stable** when there exist constants $C\ge1$, $\alpha>0$, and $\delta>0$ such that, for all $x_0$ with $d(x_0,x^\ast)<\delta$, the forward trajectory is supported and

$$
d(\phi_t(x_0),x^\ast)
\le
C e^{-\alpha t}d(x_0,x^\ast),
\qquad t\ge0.
$$

This quantitative bound implies Lyapunov stability and local attraction under the stated metric and support assumptions [@Teschl2012, sec. 6.5].
