Metric

Metric

One-sentence definition

A metric is a function assigning a nonnegative distance to each pair of points while satisfying identity, symmetry, and the triangle inequality.

Why this concept matters

Claims about proximity, neighborhoods, trajectory error, clustering, stability, and geometry depend on a notion of distance. Coordinates alone do not determine which metric is scientifically meaningful.

Intuition

A metric turns “near” and “far” into declared mathematical relations. Different metrics on the same set can produce different neighborhoods and nearest neighbors.

Formal definition

For a set \(X\), a metric is a function

\[ d:X\times X\to[0,\infty) \]

such that for all \(x,y,z\in X\):

\[ d(x,y)=0\iff x=y, \]

\[ d(x,y)=d(y,x), \]

\[ d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z). \]

These are identity of indiscernibles, symmetry, and the triangle inequality. Nonnegativity is encoded in the codomain. Sutherland states the same metric axioms and the induced topology (Sutherland 2024).

Balls, topology, and convergence

The open ball of radius \(r>0\) around \(x\) is

\[ B_r^d(x)=\{y\in X:d(x,y)<r\}. \]

Open balls form a basis for the topology induced by \(d\). A sequence converges to \(x\) exactly when

\[ d(x_n,x)\to0. \]

It is Cauchy when

\[ \forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists N\;\forall m,n\ge N: \quad d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon. \]

The metric space is complete when every Cauchy sequence converges to a point in \(X\). Completeness depends on the metric, not only on the underlying topology.

Norms and inner products

On a vector space, every norm induces the translation-invariant metric

\[ d(x,y)=\|x-y\|. \]

An inner product induces the norm \(\|x\|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}\) and therefore a metric. Not every norm comes from an inner product, and not every metric comes from a norm.

Squared Euclidean distance,

\[ d_2^2(x,y)=\|x-y\|_2^2, \]

is generally not a metric because it can violate the triangle inequality. On \(\mathbb R\), for example, \(d_2^2(0,2)=4>1+1=d_2^2(0,1)+d_2^2(1,2)\).

Equivalent metrics

Two metrics are topologically equivalent when they induce the same open sets. They then have the same convergent sequences and continuous maps, but need not have the same Cauchy sequences, completeness, numerical distances, nearest neighbors, or convergence rates.

They are bi-Lipschitz equivalent when there exist constants \(0<c\le C<\infty\) such that

\[ c\,d_1(x,y) \le d_2(x,y) \le C\,d_1(x,y) \]

for all \(x,y\). Bi-Lipschitz equivalence is quantitative: it preserves Cauchy behavior and completeness and controls distortion of distances up to constants. It is stronger than topological equivalence.

Product and weighted metrics

If \((X_1,d_1)\) and \((X_2,d_2)\) are metric spaces, examples on \(X_1\times X_2\) include

\[ d_{\max}((x_1,x_2),(y_1,y_2)) = \max\{d_1(x_1,y_1),d_2(x_2,y_2)\}, \]

and

\[ d_2((x_1,x_2),(y_1,y_2)) = \sqrt{d_1(x_1,y_1)^2+d_2(x_2,y_2)^2}. \]

For \(x,y\in\mathbb R^D\) and weights \(w_i>0\),

\[ d_w(x,y) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{D}w_i(x_i-y_i)^2} \]

is a metric. Zero weights produce only a pseudometric on the full coordinate space because distinct points can have zero distance. Negative weights do not define a real positive metric.

Rescaling coordinates and then applying ordinary Euclidean distance changes the metric unless the metric is transformed consistently. Coordinate invariance is therefore a property to demonstrate, not an automatic consequence of using coordinates.

Metric-space and Riemannian metrics

A Riemannian Metric assigns inner products to tangent spaces and induces curve lengths and a point-to-point distance by taking an infimum over path lengths. The tensor field and the induced metric-space distance are related but are not the same mathematical object (Lee 1997, chs. 3, 6).

Core notation

  • \((X,d)\): metric space.
  • \(d(x,y)\): distance.
  • \(B_r^d(x)=\{y:d(x,y)<r\}\): open metric ball.
  • \(x_n\to x\): metric convergence, equivalent to \(d(x_n,x)\to0\).
  • \(d_1,d_2\): alternative metrics on the same set.
  • \(c,C\): bi-Lipschitz lower and upper distortion constants.
  • \(d_w\): weighted Euclidean metric with strictly positive weights.

What this does NOT imply

A metric does not imply Euclidean geometry, smoothness, completeness, a unique scientific choice, coordinate invariance, causal similarity, or that nearest points share mechanism. Intrinsic-dimension estimates, neighborhood graphs, and local geometric summaries can change with the selected metric and scale. Topological equivalence does not imply equal nearest neighbors, Cauchy behavior, completeness, or quantitative rates.

Minimal example

On \(\mathbb R^2\),

\[ d_E(x,y)=\sqrt{(x_1-y_1)^2+(x_2-y_2)^2} \]

and

\[ d_M(x,y)=|x_1-y_1|+|x_2-y_2| \]

are both metrics and induce the standard topology, but their balls are circular and diamond-shaped and their numerical distances differ. The weighted metric

\[ d_w(x,y)=\sqrt{100(x_1-y_1)^2+(x_2-y_2)^2} \]

makes differences in the first coordinate ten times more costly. A nearest-neighbor or trajectory-error analysis can therefore change solely because of the declared metric.

Neuroscience / behavioral relevance

Distances between neural states or behaviors often depend on scaling, covariance, temporal alignment, and noise. Metric choice should be justified rather than inherited silently from plotting coordinates.

Relevance to LDG

At L2, Metric supplies the explicit notions of topology, convergence, completeness, quantitative equivalence, and coordinate weighting needed for stability, geometry, neighbors, and trajectory comparisons. It makes metric dependence a reviewable scientific assumption rather than a plotting default.

# Metric

## One-sentence definition

A **metric** is a function assigning a nonnegative distance to each pair of points while satisfying identity, symmetry, and the triangle inequality.

## Why this concept matters

Claims about proximity, neighborhoods, trajectory error, clustering, stability, and geometry depend on a notion of distance. Coordinates alone do not determine which metric is scientifically meaningful.

## Intuition

A metric turns “near” and “far” into declared mathematical relations. Different metrics on the same set can produce different neighborhoods and nearest neighbors.

## Formal definition

For a set $X$, a metric is a function

$$
d:X\times X\to[0,\infty)
$$

such that for all $x,y,z\in X$:

$$
d(x,y)=0\iff x=y,
$$

$$
d(x,y)=d(y,x),
$$

$$
d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z).
$$

These are identity of indiscernibles, symmetry, and the triangle inequality. Nonnegativity is encoded in the codomain. Sutherland states the same metric axioms and the induced topology [@Sutherland2024].

### Balls, topology, and convergence

The open ball of radius $r>0$ around $x$ is

$$
B_r^d(x)=\{y\in X:d(x,y)<r\}.
$$

Open balls form a basis for the topology induced by $d$. A sequence converges to $x$ exactly when

$$
d(x_n,x)\to0.
$$

It is Cauchy when

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists N\;\forall m,n\ge N:
\quad d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon.
$$

The metric space is complete when every Cauchy sequence converges to a point in $X$. Completeness depends on the metric, not only on the underlying topology.

### Norms and inner products

On a vector space, every norm induces the translation-invariant metric

$$
d(x,y)=\|x-y\|.
$$

An inner product induces the norm $\|x\|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}$ and therefore a metric. Not every norm comes from an inner product, and not every metric comes from a norm.

Squared Euclidean distance,

$$
d_2^2(x,y)=\|x-y\|_2^2,
$$

is generally not a metric because it can violate the triangle inequality. On $\mathbb R$, for example, $d_2^2(0,2)=4>1+1=d_2^2(0,1)+d_2^2(1,2)$.

### Equivalent metrics

Two metrics are **topologically equivalent** when they induce the same open sets. They then have the same convergent sequences and continuous maps, but need not have the same Cauchy sequences, completeness, numerical distances, nearest neighbors, or convergence rates.

They are **bi-Lipschitz equivalent** when there exist constants $0<c\le C<\infty$ such that

$$
c\,d_1(x,y)
\le
d_2(x,y)
\le
C\,d_1(x,y)
$$

for all $x,y$. Bi-Lipschitz equivalence is quantitative: it preserves Cauchy behavior and completeness and controls distortion of distances up to constants. It is stronger than topological equivalence.

### Product and weighted metrics

If $(X_1,d_1)$ and $(X_2,d_2)$ are metric spaces, examples on $X_1\times X_2$ include

$$
d_{\max}((x_1,x_2),(y_1,y_2))
=
\max\{d_1(x_1,y_1),d_2(x_2,y_2)\},
$$

and

$$
d_2((x_1,x_2),(y_1,y_2))
=
\sqrt{d_1(x_1,y_1)^2+d_2(x_2,y_2)^2}.
$$

For $x,y\in\mathbb R^D$ and weights $w_i>0$,

$$
d_w(x,y)
=
\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{D}w_i(x_i-y_i)^2}
$$

is a metric. Zero weights produce only a pseudometric on the full coordinate space because distinct points can have zero distance. Negative weights do not define a real positive metric.

### Metric-space and Riemannian metrics

A [Riemannian Metric](riemannian-metric.qmd) assigns inner products to tangent spaces and induces curve lengths and a point-to-point distance by taking an infimum over path lengths. The tensor field and the induced metric-space distance are related but are not the same mathematical object [@Lee1997, chs. 3, 6].
