Riemannian Metric

Riemannian Metric

One-sentence definition

A Riemannian metric assigns a positive-definite inner product to every tangent space of a smooth manifold, varying smoothly from point to point.

Why this concept matters

A smooth manifold supplies local coordinates but no canonical lengths or angles. A Riemannian metric adds the structure needed to measure tangent vectors, curve length, angle, volume, geodesic distance, and curvature.

Intuition

At each point, place a smoothly changing ruler and protractor on the tangent space. The rulers may stretch different directions by different amounts.

Formal definition

Let \(M\) be a smooth \(n\)-manifold. A Riemannian metric is a smooth symmetric positive-definite covariant \(2\)-tensor field

\[ g\in\Gamma(S^2T^*M). \]

Equivalently, each \(p\in M\) receives an inner product

\[ g_p:T_pM\times T_pM\to\mathbb R \]

such that \(p\mapsto g_p(X_p,Y_p)\) is smooth for smooth vector fields \(X,Y\). Symmetry and positive definiteness mean

\[ g_p(v,w)=g_p(w,v), \qquad g_p(v,v)>0\quad(v\ne0). \]

Lee gives this definition and its elementary constructions (Lee 1997, ch. 3).

Coordinate representation

In coordinates \(x=(x^1,\ldots,x^n)\),

\[ g=g_{ij}(x)\,dx^i\otimes dx^j, \]

where \([g_{ij}(x)]\) is a smooth symmetric positive-definite matrix. Under coordinates \(y=y(x)\),

\[ g'_{ab}(y) = \frac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a} \frac{\partial x^j}{\partial y^b} g_{ij}(x(y)). \]

Thus the component matrix changes by the tensor transformation law; the geometric metric does not equal one fixed matrix independent of coordinates.

Tangent norms and angles

For \(v,w\in T_pM\),

\[ \|v\|_{g,p}=\sqrt{g_p(v,v)}, \]

and for nonzero vectors

\[ \cos\angle_g(v,w) = \frac{g_p(v,w)}{\|v\|_{g,p}\|w\|_{g,p}}. \]

Orthogonality means \(g_p(v,w)=0\). These quantities compare vectors in the same tangent space; comparing vectors at different points requires additional transport or coordinates.

Curve length and induced distance

For a piecewise smooth curve \(\gamma:[a,b]\to M\),

\[ L_g(\gamma) = \int_a^b \sqrt{g_{\gamma(t)}(\dot\gamma(t),\dot\gamma(t))} \,dt. \]

This length is invariant under orientation-preserving reparameterizations. On a connected component, the induced Riemannian distance is

\[ d_g(p,q) = \inf_{\gamma:p\to q}L_g(\gamma), \]

where the infimum ranges over piecewise smooth curves joining \(p\) and \(q\). Lee shows that this is a metric on a connected Riemannian manifold (Lee 1997, ch. 6). Points in distinct connected components have no joining curve; one either treats distance componentwise or uses an extended distance \(+\infty\).

The infimum need not initially be attained, and the existence or uniqueness of a minimizing geodesic requires additional conditions. The Geodesic object records the local relationship without assuming global minimizers.

Pullback and induced metrics

For a smooth map \(F:M\to N\) and a Riemannian metric \(h\) on \(N\), the pullback tensor is

\[ (F^*h)_p(v,w) = h_{F(p)}(dF_pv,dF_pw). \]

It is symmetric and nonnegative. It is positive definite—and hence a Riemannian metric on \(M\)—when \(dF_p\) is injective for every \(p\), for example when \(F\) is an immersion. For an embedded submanifold, the inclusion pulls the ambient metric back to the standard induced metric (Lee 1997, ch. 3). An arbitrary non-injective map can yield a degenerate pullback and does not automatically define a Riemannian metric.

A diffeomorphism \(F:(M,g)\to(N,h)\) is an isometry when \(F^*h=g\). Isometric descriptions preserve the Riemannian lengths, angles, and induced distances even though coordinate matrices may differ.

Core notation

  • \(g\): Riemannian metric.
  • \(g_p(v,w)=\langle v,w\rangle_p\): inner product at \(p\).
  • \(\|v\|_p=\sqrt{g_p(v,v)}\): tangent-vector norm.
  • \(L_g(\gamma)\): curve length induced by \(g\).
  • \(d_g(p,q)\): point distance obtained as an infimum of curve lengths.
  • \(g_{ij}\): coordinate matrix of metric components.
  • \(F^*h\): pullback metric/tensor.
  • \(dF_p\): differential used to test pullback nondegeneracy.

Prerequisites

Metric supplies distance language. Tangent Space supplies the vector spaces receiving inner products.

Relationship to adjacent concepts

Concept Distinction
Metric-space metric Directly assigns point-to-point distance; a Riemannian metric first acts on tangent vectors and induces distance through path lengths.
Euclidean metric A constant special case in Euclidean coordinates.
Mahalanobis distance A fixed positive-definite quadratic metric on a vector space, corresponding to a constant Riemannian metric in those coordinates; semidefinite matrices yield only pseudodistance.
Pseudo-Riemannian metric Uses a nondegenerate but indefinite bilinear form and need not define ordinary positive distance.
Sub-Riemannian metric Defines inner products only on a selected tangent distribution; admissible path length and distance require additional bracket/accessibility structure.
Finsler metric Uses a smoothly varying norm that need not arise from a quadratic inner product.
Learned metric A fitted local or global distance/tensor model whose positivity, smoothness, identifiability, and generalization require validation.

What this does NOT imply

A Riemannian metric does not imply zero curvature, completeness, unique coordinates, a unique data-derived choice, global geodesic existence or uniqueness, an isometric embedding in a chosen ambient space, or biological meaning. Positive-definite fitted matrices at sampled points do not by themselves establish a smooth global metric field.

Minimal example

On \(\mathbb R^2\), the Euclidean metric is

\[ g=dx^2+dy^2. \]

If \(a(x,y)>0\) is smooth, the position-dependent metric \(g=a(x,y)^2dx^2+dy^2\) assigns different local costs to motion in the \(x\) direction.

Neuroscience / behavioral relevance

A learned local metric can encode anisotropic variability or task sensitivity in latent space. It must be evaluated against simpler fixed metrics and should not be interpreted mechanistically from fit alone.

Relevance to LDG

At L2, Riemannian Metric supplies typed coordinate transformations, tangent norms/angles, curve lengths, induced distance, pullback conditions, and isometry language needed for geodesics and curvature without conflating nonlinear embeddings with geometry.

$$
